# Stellar dApp Legal Compliance Tutorial

Shipping a Stellar dApp — especially one that moves value, issues assets, or serves users across jurisdictions — puts you in contact with legal areas that have nothing to do with Soroban or Horizon. This tutorial is not legal advice. It is a map of the categories you are likely to run into, so you know what to ask a lawyer about before you need to.

---

## Why this matters for a Stellar dApp specifically

Stellar's design choices raise compliance questions earlier than a typical web app does:

- **Asset issuance** (`stellar-sdk`'s `Asset` / trustline flows) can look like issuing a security or a stored-value instrument depending on what the asset represents.
- **Cross-border payments** are Stellar's core use case, which means money-transmission and sanctions rules are relevant from day one, not as an afterthought.
- **Non-custodial wallets** (Freighter, Albedo, xBull) shift some liability away from you, but only if your dApp genuinely never custodies user funds or keys.
- **Public ledger data** means every transaction your dApp submits is permanently, publicly visible — this affects data-protection analysis in ways a private database doesn't.

None of this means you need a legal team before your first commit. It means you should know which of the categories below apply to what you're building.

---

## Common categories

### 1. Money transmission and licensing

If your dApp facilitates transfers of value between users — even just relaying signed transactions — you may fall under money-transmitter regulations in some jurisdictions (e.g., US state-level MTL requirements, EU's PSD2/MiCA). This depends heavily on:

- Whether you ever touch user funds (custodial vs. non-custodial)
- Whether you're matching buyers/sellers or just broadcasting transactions
- Where your users are located

### 2. Securities and token classification

If your dApp issues, wraps, or trades an asset, ask whether that asset could be classified as a security under your jurisdiction's test (e.g., the US Howey test). Utility tokens, stablecoins pegged to fiat, and yield-bearing assets each carry different analysis.

### 3. AML / KYC obligations

Anti-money-laundering rules can apply based on transaction volume, whether you operate an exchange-like function, or whether you're classified as a Virtual Asset Service Provider (VASP) under frameworks like FATF's Travel Rule.

### 4. Sanctions compliance

Public blockchains don't have built-in sanctions screening. If your dApp interacts with counterparties you don't control (e.g., an open DEX), you should understand whether — and how — sanctions list screening applies to your specific product.

### 5. Data protection

Off-chain data (emails, KYC documents, session data) is subject to normal data-protection law (GDPR, CCPA, etc.). On-chain data is public and immutable, which has its own implications — you generally cannot honor a "right to be forgotten" request for data already committed to a public ledger.

### 6. Terms of service and liability

Non-custodial dApps typically disclaim custody and control over user funds explicitly in their terms. This is a legal drafting exercise, not a code change, but it directly shapes how you should design consent flows in the UI.

## When to seek counsel

Treat these as trigger points, not a complete list:

- Before you launch any feature that issues a new asset or token.
- Before you accept fiat on-ramps or off-ramps.
- Before you operate anything resembling an order book, matching engine, or liquidity pool with your own capital.
- Before you expand to users in a new country.
- If you are ever unsure whether your dApp is "custodial" in a given flow — this is a legal question, not just a technical one.
- Before writing your terms of service and privacy policy for production.

A short paid consultation with a lawyer familiar with digital assets, early, is far cheaper than a compliance retrofit later.

---

## A starting checklist

Use this as a conversation starter with counsel, not a substitute for one:

- [ ] Have we identified every flow where the dApp could be considered to custody user funds or keys?
- [ ] Have we classified every asset our dApp issues, wraps, or lists?
- [ ] Do we know which jurisdictions our users are in, and which of those have money-transmission or VASP licensing regimes?
- [ ] Have we documented whether we perform any KYC/AML function, even indirectly?
- [ ] Does our terms of service disclaim custody and describe the non-custodial nature of the product accurately?
- [ ] Have we reviewed what user data we collect off-chain and how it's protected?
- [ ] Do we have a process for responding to law-enforcement or regulatory inquiries about on-chain activity?

## Verifying this tutorial stays accurate

Compliance requirements change faster than most documentation. Before relying on this page:

- Confirm the categories above still reflect current regulatory guidance in your target jurisdictions — cite the specific regulator (e.g., FinCEN, FCA, MiCA text) rather than this page, when in doubt.
- Re-review after any material change to your product (new asset type, new country, new custody model).
- Treat every "when to seek counsel" trigger point as non-negotiable, not optional.
